# Synthesis

## Question 1, tree and stand attributes

Established. The spruce beetle takes large mature spruce first. Massey and Wygant (1954, p. 13) wrote that the Engelmann spruce beetle "prefers the large, more mature trees", and that as the large trees were depleted the beetles moved to smaller diameter classes; the average diameter of infested trees in the White River outbreak fell from 21 to 17 to 15 inches (Schmid and Frye 1976, p. 2). In Alaska, Werner and Holsten (1983, p. 96) found that "Mortality was greatest in the larger diameter classes during the early part of the infestation, but smaller diameter trees were subsequently attacked as the number of noninfested trees declined", Baker and Kemperman (1974, p. 423) that survival "was greater in smaller diameters", and Doak (2004, p. 810) in the Copper River basin that "Mortality increased with increasing DBH and decreasing BAI". Hard et al. (1983, p. 678) qualified the diameter effect, "Diameter was not an important criterion for spruce susceptibility to attack or death unless large diameter was coupled with slower than average radial growth in the last 5 years", and Hard (1985, p. 839) showed that attacked and killed trees grew more slowly than unattacked trees while trees killed and trees unsuccessfully attacked "did not differ in diameter". The Forest Service synthesis of that work states the rule in one sentence, "Spruce beetles preferentially attack and kill large diameter trees that have been growing slowly at breast height" (Hard and Holsten 1985, p. 7). Hazard ratings carried the rule to the stand. Schmid and Frye (1976, p. 1) rated stands on "physiographic location, tree diameter, basal area, and percentage of spruce in the canopy", Reynolds and Holsten (1994, p. 1015) built a decision tree on total basal area, the spruce share of basal area, the share of spruce basal area in trees over 25 cm, elevation and aspect, and the current leaflet gives the thresholds in use, stands with average spruce diameters over 40 cm, basal areas over 34 square metres per hectare and a live spruce component over 60 per cent in drainage bottoms (Fettig et al. 2026, p. 8).

Confounds. Growth rate and diameter are confounded in a stand, because the large trees of an overstocked stand grow slowly (Hard and Holsten 1985, p. 6). Host species matters within Alaska, Lutz spruce suffering more frequent and severe outbreaks than white or Sitka spruce (Holsten and Werner 1990, p. 292). Beetle population phase changes host selection, since only eruptive populations colonise defended healthy trees (Wallin and Raffa 2004, p. 101). Stand age and fire history set susceptibility at the landscape scale (Veblen et al. 1991; Kulakowski and Veblen 2006; Bakaj et al. 2016).

Effect sizes. Hard et al. (1983) related the sigmoid of per cent mortality to the number of spruce per hectare above 24.1 cm; Werner and Holsten (1983) recorded 29 per cent of white spruce killed over 1976 to 1980 and 59 per cent of commercial volume; Reynolds and Holsten (1994) predicted high hazard correctly for 73 per cent of stands; Massey and Wygant (1954) recorded 75 per cent of the 36 to 38 inch trees killed by 1943.

Where this study sits. The study does not test the rule, which rests on ground plots across seventy years. It carries the rule to a map, taking stand height as the satellite measure of the large dominant trees the beetle takes, and asks whether that measure ranks the stands an outbreak killed where the Zwieback et al. (2024) crowns say which ones died. This is an extension from plot to map, not a replication.

## Question 2, remote sensing of spruce beetle damage

Established. The aerial detection survey is the operational record (Reynolds and Holsten 1994 named it among the risk factors through neighbouring infestations). Landsat change methods map severe mortality and miss the low-severity stands in which most dead trees stand (Zwieback et al. 2024, pp. 412 and 419); Ye et al. (2021) and Woodward et al. (2018) mapped spruce beetle mortality in Colorado from dense Landsat series and composites; Hart and Veblen (2015) compared high and medium resolution imagery for the same task. In Alaska, Cessna et al. (2021, abstract) reached "78% accuracy for all three health statuses" and 97 per cent for non-infested against dead crowns from airborne hyperspectral and lidar data. Radar had scarcely been used for insect damage before Sentinel-1, and shorter wavelengths have shown more potential than L-band (Hollaus and Vreugdenhil 2019, Conclusions 2 and 4); Hechtl et al. (2025) fitted Sentinel-1 features to a vitality score in Bavaria with the leakage noted in its source note.

Confounds. Detection studies measure mortality after the event, and their accuracy depends on severity (Rodman et al. 2021). The survey polygons are sketched at a scale at which a low-severity polygon is mostly living forest.

Effect sizes. Zwieback et al. (2024) found the Landsat relation to severity emerging only above 0.1; Cessna et al. (2021) 78 and 97 per cent.

Where this study sits. No retained record measured Sentinel-1 or canopy height against spruce beetle mortality at the stand scale on areas held out whole. The study reports the first such numbers, and finds that they measure the stand before the outbreak rather than the mortality.

## Question 3, novelty

No retained record produced a regional or statewide map of spruce stands susceptible to the spruce beetle from satellite structure data and tested it against the outbreak. Every hazard rating in the corpus rests on a cruise (Schmid and Frye 1976; Reynolds and Holsten 1994; Reynolds and Holsten 1996; Fettig et al. 2026), and the occurrence model of Zabihi et al. (2021) predicts beetle habitat rather than host stands. The absence was checked against the full text of the five records read in full that bear on mapping (Zwieback et al. 2024; Woodward et al. 2018; Ye et al. 2021; Cessna et al. 2021; Hollaus and Vreugdenhil 2019) and against the titles and abstracts of the 16 remaining retained question 2 records. The study is new work on that count, and is stated as such.
